What Counts as Non-Shielded Arc Welding in New Orleans?

In New Orleans, non-shielded arc welding is the term we use for wire-fed arc work done without a shielding gas cylinder. The weld still needs protection from the air, but it comes from flux inside the wire, or from a blanket of granular flux in submerged arc welding, rather than from a gas cylinder.

  • Self-Shielded Flux-Cored Wire

    This is the process we bring to your site. Flux packed inside a hollow wire burns in the arc and forms a gas that protects the molten pool, so no cylinder, hose or regulator is needed.

  • Submerged Arc Welding

    The arc runs buried under a layer of granular flux, so it cannot be seen. It is usually run by a machine on long, flat seams in a shop, which makes it a production process rather than a mobile one.

  • Bare Electrodes, Where It Started

    Early metal arc welding used bare wire with nothing to keep the air out, and the welds were often brittle. Electrodes with a flux coating began replacing bare wire in the early 1900s for that reason.

  • Gas-Shielded Processes Compared

    Stick welding carries its shield in a flux coating on the rod, while MIG and TIG draw gas from a cylinder. We cover all three on our shielded arc welding page.

Why Does Non-Shielded Arc Welding Work Well Outdoors in New Orleans?

Non-shielded arc welding works well outdoors in New Orleans because there is no shielding gas for the wind to blow away. Self-shielded flux-cored wire carries its protection inside, so it suits fences, docks and other exposed steel, and it is more forgiving of light rust and mill scale than solid MIG wire.

Wind is the main reason gas-shielded welds fail outdoors, and gasless wire takes that problem away.

When a breeze pushes shielding gas off the joint, air reaches the weld and leaves it full of pores. Self-shielded wire makes its own protective gas right at the arc, so it usually stays reliable in open air without the windscreens a gas process may need.

That makes it a practical fit for waterfront steel, where wind and salt are constant. Our guide on marine and dockside welding covers the extra care those structures need.

What Are the Benefits of Non-Shielded Arc Welding in New Orleans?

The benefits of non-shielded arc welding in New Orleans are speed, lower material needs and portability. Flux-cored wire feeds continuously and lays down metal quickly, no gas has to be bought or hauled to the site, and the wire handles thicker steel well. The tradeoff is slag and fumes to manage.

  • Fast, Continuous Welding

    The wire feeds from a spool, so there is no stopping to change rods. Flux-cored wire also deposits metal at a high rate, which keeps longer welds moving.

  • Fewer Materials to Bring

    There is no gas cylinder to buy, fill or carry. That keeps the setup lean and the job focused on the weld.

  • Suited to Thicker Steel

    Self-shielded wire is usually a good match for heavier sections such as frames, posts and structural parts. Very thin sheet usually calls for a different process.

What Do New Orleans Customers Want to Know About Non-Shielded Arc Welding?

We answer the gasless welding questions New Orleans customers ask most: what gasless flux core welding is called, whether it needs gas, how it compares with MIG and stick, what causes spatter, what it is used for, where it falls short and which direction to weld.

What is gasless flux core welding called?

Gasless flux core welding is called self-shielded flux-cored arc welding, shortened to FCAW-S. Many welders simply call it gasless wire or gasless MIG, after the machines it often runs on. The flux inside the wire creates the shielding gas, so no cylinder is connected to the machine.

What gas do you use for flux core welding?

Self-shielded flux core welding uses no gas at all, because the flux in the wire makes its own shielding. Gas-shielded flux-cored wire is a separate type that does need gas, usually carbon dioxide or an argon and carbon dioxide mix. Always check the wire label, since the two must not be mixed up.

Is MIG welding the same as flux core?

Not quite, although both feed wire from a spool through a gun and many machines can run either one. MIG uses a solid wire with shielding gas from a cylinder, while flux core uses a hollow wire filled with flux. Flux core leaves slag to chip off, and MIG usually leaves a cleaner bead.

What causes spatter when flux core welding?

Spatter in flux core welding usually comes from the wrong polarity, the wrong stickout or voltage and wire speed that do not match the wire. Most self-shielded wires run on DC electrode negative, so a machine left set for MIG often spatters badly. Some wires also spatter more than others.

Which is easier, stick welding or flux core?

Flux core is usually easier to learn than stick welding. The wire feeds itself, so you only manage travel speed and angle, and there is no rod to strike or replace. Stick takes more practice to start and hold an arc, though both processes leave slag and both handle outdoor work.

What is flux core welding used for?

Flux core welding is used for outdoor repairs, structural steel, farm and heavy equipment, and work on thicker steel where speed matters. It is popular in the field because self-shielded wire handles wind and somewhat dirty metal. It is mainly used on carbon steel rather than on thin sheet.

Why is flux core welding limited?

Flux core welding has limits because of the flux. It leaves slag that must be removed between passes, it produces more smoke and fumes than MIG, and the bead is usually less neat. It also suits thicker steel better than very thin sheet, and it is not the usual choice for aluminum.

Do you push or pull flux core welding?

You pull, or drag, when flux core welding, following the old rule that if there is slag, you drag. Pulling the gun lets the slag form behind the weld pool instead of getting pushed ahead and trapped in the bead. A drag angle of about 5 to 15 degrees is a common starting point.
